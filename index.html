About

I am a Computer Science PhD student at UC Santa Barbara, advised by Prof. Tobias Höllerer in the Four Eyes Lab. My research is on modulating saliency to affect human cognition in augmented and virtual reality (AR and VR), whether through conventional highlighting techniques or through AI that modulates saliency without losing information in the scene. I am the lead developer and experimenter on an ongoing Magic Leap 2 study in DARPA's Intrinsic Cognitive Security program, and I built its study app in Unity and OpenXR with room calibration and head, eye and controller tracking. My first-author paper on modeling object attention in mobile AR won the Best Paper Award at the ACM MobiHoc 2025 extended reality (XR) security workshop, and a paper on object recall in an AR office scene, where I am co-second author, received an Honorable Mention at IEEE ISMAR 2026 (TVCG). Before graduate school I served four years as a US Army airborne infantryman, then earned my B.S. in computer science magna cum laude at Cal Poly Pomona, where I did research in computer vision, deep learning and anomaly detection.

Modeling Object Attention in Mobile AR

My first-author paper models object recall in mobile AR with partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM), pooling 1,152 recall events from four studies. It identifies lighting, augmentation density, AR registration stability, cognitive load and AR familiarity as primary drivers of recall and won the Best Paper Award at the ACM MobiHoc 2025 extended reality (XR) security workshop.

GUARD: Detecting Unknown Attacks with Graph-Based Deep Learning

GUARD (Graph-based Unknown Attack Recognition and Detection), my first-author paper at the IEEE International Workshop on Information Forensics and Security (WIFS) 2024, detects attack classes the model never saw in training. An autoencoder's latent representations and reconstruction error become the vertices of a graph with cosine-similarity edges, and a new Hierarchical Quartet Loss separates attacks and attack families within it. On the NSL-KDD dataset it averaged 0.9163 recall on unknown attacks.

The team built and flight-tested two unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) for autonomous search and rescue where the Global Positioning System (GPS) is unavailable. Diffusion Planning vs. Diffusion Policy

Our team of four ran one diffusion model on the HalfCheetah simulation task in two ways, as a policy and as a planner that samples 16 action sequences, tries each in the simulator and acts on the best one. I built the framework, including the data pipeline, the diffusion models and samplers, both modes and the evaluation. The planner averaged a return of 14,117 against 11,988 for the policy.

Gaze-CAM

Gaze-CAM compares where four video action-recognition networks look with where the actor looked, using egocentric video from the EGTEA Gaze+ dataset and Grad-CAM, a method that maps which parts of the input drove a network's decision. I built the pipeline that trains R3D-18, SlowFast, TimeSformer and ViViT, computes their Grad-CAM maps and scores them against gaze. TimeSformer's attention matched gaze most closely, while SlowFast scored below a center-bias baseline despite 59.8% accuracy.

Graph Neural Network Surrogate for Plasma Simulation

In a course project with three classmates, I worked on hybrid graph neural network (GNN) flux integrators that simulate one-dimensional fluid-Poisson plasma dynamics. I ran the ablation study across 12 hybrid models, created the upwind flux and forward Euler physics solver, and organized the project's GitHub repository. The hybrid took 5.5 ms per time step against 18.7 ms for the third-order Runge-Kutta (RK3) and Lax-Friedrichs solver, and its best configuration ended 100 time steps with about half the energy drift of the forward Euler solver.

Ahead-of-Time vs. Just-in-Time Compilation for Deep Learning

Ahead-of-time compilers optimize a whole model before it runs, and just-in-time compilers optimize it while it runs. With a teammate, I measured when either one pays off for running a trained model. We took two image networks (LeNet-5 and ResNet-18) and two recurrent networks, a long short-term memory (LSTM) network and a gated recurrent unit (GRU) network, and ran each through five execution engines, from plain PyTorch with no compiler (eager mode) to TensorRT. We recorded speed, graphics card (GPU) memory and energy at four batch sizes. I trained and validated the four models, wrote the TorchScript conversion and the just-in-time TorchScript engine for the benchmark, and added the significance tests and summary charts. At batch size 1, TensorRT ran the image networks about twice as fast as eager mode (2.12 and 2.00 times) but ran the recurrent networks at less than half of eager speed (0.42 and 0.44 times). At batch size 128 it was slower than eager mode on the image networks as well.

droneGAT

In this course project with a teammate, 16 simulated drones fly in formation in the AirSim simulator and form a graph. A graph attention network (GAT) passes each drone's camera features to nearby drones, adjusted for their relative position and orientation, and each drone then predicts a depth map and a per-pixel class map (semantic segmentation) for its own view. I wrote the model and the training, testing and analysis code. With the cameras of 5 of the 16 drones corrupted by motion blur or noise, the best GAT run scored 0.70 mean intersection over union (mIoU, a 0 to 1 score of how well the predicted and true regions overlap) and 82.5% pixel accuracy on segmentation, level with the best run of the cross-attention model it was compared against (0.70 and 82.6%).

Wildfire Spread Prediction

Given limited imagery from high altitude cameras and sensors, this project aims to both detect wildfires and predict fire spread.

In this video, the smoke detection model outputs a mask, dilates it, and uses sparse optical flow. Given the camera angle, the Ground Sampling Distance (GSD), and framerate, the system can determine wind speed and direction. This then is fed into the overall fire spread prediction model.

I also built a program that turns burned-area maps from historical fires into finer time steps and fed that data into a digital twin in NVIDIA Omniverse, shown in the second image.
